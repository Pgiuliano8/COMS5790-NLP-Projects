A load of atoms could be solid, liquid or gas.

One is called experiment. 
The other is called direct observation. 

To know more about experiments, it’s important to know how to design them. 
That’s the whole idea of clinical trials, 
or agricultural experiments,
or psychology experiments. 
For the purpose of this class, we don’t have time to really explain experiments. 
Actually, you can take courses about it, these courses are usually called “research methods.”
For now, you just have to know that there’s something called experiments.

Another way of collecting data is direct observation. 
And people do direct observation studies all the time. 
What would be the simplest, 
most common type of direct observation?

It’s a survey. 
There are three types of observational studies:
Cross-sectional studies ,
Retrospective studies ,
And prospective studies .
And they all involve time in a different way. 

Cross-sectional study is the most common. 
Here, you take a measurement at a given time and you look at the data. 

For example, if you want to know the height of men and women in your country, 
you survey a sample of men and a sample of women. 

The data shows you people’s height 
at the time you collected the data. 
So you’re essentially stopping time, 
and you compare data across groups. 


Retrospective studies go back in time. 
You’re asking questions like “what happened historically?”

Here’s an example. 
Robert Fogel is an American historian and he was interested in Civil War medical records. 

After the Civil war, the U.S. government gave medical benefits to war veterans. 
If you were a Civil War survivor, you could collect some benefits. 
But the benefits you get depend on the evaluation you got from the government doctors. 
So someone who ended up in worse shape usually got more benefits. 
Because of this policy, the national archives has these huge medical records from all of these people, 
and the government tracked how they were over time. 
Such as how long they lived, 
what diseases they had, 
etc etc. 
And Robert Fogel wanted to know what happened to these people. 

So, he hired a bunch of researchers at the national archives to go back in time and see what happened. 
This is a retrospective study because he was going back in time and looking at existing data – 
data that have already been collected, 
perhaps data collected by someone else!

In fact, a lot of retrospective studies are not done by the people who collected the data. 
For example, Facebook makes some of their data publicly available. 
Maybe 10 years from now you become a data scientist, 
and you want to analyze these Facebook data for a specific purpose. 
That would be a retrospective study. 

Now let’s talk about prospective studies. 
Economists like to call these longitudinal data 
- you’ll also see these in the psychology or medical literature. 

Prospective means go forward in time.
Here’s an example, a health study follows 20,000 nurses over a long period of time, 
the nurses fill out a medical form every 6 months or so.
The researchers were collecting data forward in time . 
Unlike retrospective studies, the person who collects the data in a prospective study is usually the person who designed the study in the first place. 

So retrospective studies and prospective studies are really the opposite sides of the same coin. 

So, these are the three types of observational studies. Stop time, go backward in time, go forward in time. 

Observational studies just say you observe what’s going on, and you’re not affecting the action at all. 
For example, if you wanna know something about crickets, 
and you wonder whether female crickets choose their mates based on health, 
you should just observe but not interfere. 
That’s an observational study. 
You’re observing the action but you’re not having a part in what’s going on. 

The key about observational studies is that you’re collecting data as you observe. 
But you could be in trouble if you forget to collect some variables. 
And you can never show causation in observational studies, 
because you might have what’s called a lurking variable or a confounding variable. 

So when you do a survey, you have to make sure you’re asking the right questions so that you can collect the right data. 
If you don’t, there might be a confounding variable or a hidden variable that’s actually driving things but you forgot to collect it. 
And so your conclusions might be wrong because the confounding variable is driving everything. 
And you can be like, 
oh I forgot to collect those data when I did my survey. 

Now, why is confounding variable a big issue for observational studies? 

It’s because you can never really show causation, 
or cause-and-effect,
in observational studies. 
Because you can’t collect all possible variables. 
Someone could always claim, “Well the effect you’re seeing is due to age.” 
And you forgot to ask people their age when collecting the data. 
Or people could say, “Maybe the effect you’re seeing is due to location”, 
and you forgot to ask where people live. 

So what are confounding variables? 
They are hidden variables that affect your results. 

Let me give you examples. 
Everyone knows that the more a person smokes, 
the more likely they end up with lung cancer. 
It’s natural to assume that smoking causes cancer. But there was huge controversy about this in the 80s because the data are based on observational studies. 
What if people who smoke more just tend to have more bad health habits,
And those other bad health habits are what cause the cancer? 

Maybe it had nothing to do with smoking! 

Here’s another example, 
it turns out that the more firefighters show up at a scene, 
the more fire damage you get. 
Now, you probably wouldn’t assume that the firefighters are doing the damage. 
Then what’s actually causing the damage? 
 
It’s the size of the fire, and we need more firefighters when the fire is bigger. 
So the size of the fire is the confounding variable here.

Let’s do one more example, it turns out the bigger a kid’s feet, the smarter the kid is. 
But it’s not the big foot that makes the kid smart. 
What is the confounding variable here? 
 
It’s the age of the kids. 
The older the kid, the larger their feet and the smarter they are. 
So when you do observational studies, you always have to worry about confounding variables. 

In experimental studies, you actually have a hand in the action. 
So they’re different from observational studies because you’re actually running some experiment. 

Let’s use the cricket example again. 
Remember that you want to know if female crickets choose their mate based on the health of the male crickets?
The best way to answer to this question is to make of the crickets sick and then see who the female chooses. 
You’re really causing what’s going on. 
This is a basic experiment. 
 
But now, we need to think about how you can ensure that there’s no confounding variable that you didn’t consider. 
Let’s say you found out that female crickets tend to choose the healthier crickets. 
How can you be sure that the health of the male cricket is causing the effect in the experiment? 
 
Because you’ve controlled all other variables so there are no confounds. 

How do you do that? 

You randomized. 
You control for where these crickets grew up, 
how big they are, etc., 
by randomizing them. 
For example, let’s say we have three different size of crickets.
If we randomly infect half of the crickets, some big crickets will get infected and some will not. 
And the same thing will happen to the medium and small crickets. 
Because we randomly picked a bunch of crickets to get sick, 
you should end up with similar numbers of sick and healthy crickets of all sizes. 

Randomization is how we control for confounding variables. 

Really, randomization is a wonderful solution to everything, 
it's like the duct tape of statistics. 
If someone says, “Maybe health is not the cause, maybe size is.” 
You can say “No, we randomized it away.” So experiments are great. 

Just so you’re aware of, this is the father of modern day experiments. You should just know he exists. His name is Ronald Fisher, and he is considered the father of modern day statistics and experimental design. 

Doing an experiment is the gold standard to answering a question, so why do observational studies at all? 

It’s because experiments are not always easy to do. 
They can be expensive, 
they can be difficult to run, 
they can be unethical! 

The issue is that experiments are a lot of work 
and if you don't care about causality, you don't really need to run an experiment. 

For example, do I want to show that smoking is related to lung cancer? 
Or do I want to show that smoking causes lung cancer? 

Those are very different questions. 
Sometimes it’s very hard to show causality. 
Can you do a real experiment to find out if smoking causes lung cancer? 

No, because you’ll have to randomly assign some people to smoke and some not to smoke. 
That’s an unethical thing to do. 

Perhaps you’re thinking, 
“How’s this different from a vaccine trial where some people get the real vaccine and some get a placebo?”
Because in the case of smoking, you are deliberately causing harm to some people. 

In a vaccine study, most people will get a benefit and some will not. 

People aren’t supposed to be harmed in a vaccine study, 
and when people have side effects, those are unintentional. 

The medical team is not deliberately causing harm. 

That’s what separates a vaccine study from a smoking study.

So as you can see, sometimes it’s just really hard to do an experiment in practice.

Now let’s talk more about observational studies. 
Although surveys sound easy, they’re fraught with issues. 

We’re going to assume that we have simple random sampling
Or SRS. 
Again, random sampling means that 
everyone in your sample is equally likely to be picked. 
If that’s not the case, bad things can happen, 
And in the rest of this lecture I’m going to tell you what they are.

Usually, we are just going to do random sampling. 
But there’s this thing called convenience sampling. 
It’s a type of sampling in which people are sampled just because they are convenient to be picked. 
For example, you go to your dorm and ask your roommate, “Hey which team do you think will win Super Bowl this year?” That's convenience sampling. 

In stratified sampling, 
you split up the total population by strata or smaller groups. 
You ask the men a question, 
you ask the women a question, 
you split them up into different groups. 
Or you ask college students a question, 
older adults, 
or high school students. 
There are many ways to sample, but we are just going to assume we are doing simple random sampling.

The key here is that your sample needs to be unbiased. 
That’s an important word to know, 
unbiased. 

If you’re not unbiased, then what are you? 

Biased. 
You’re biased if your sample tends to favor one group over another. 
For example, you want to know who people think is going to win the superbowl.
If you just go to a male football team and give them a survey, 
your results are going to be biased 
to that particular group of individuals. 

So you have to make sure that the results are unbiased. 
Let me give you another example. 
If you want to do a survey on shopping habits 
and you do that survey to shoppers in the mall in the middle of a weekday. 
Can you generalize your results to other shoppers?

Probably not. 
But why?
You need to think about who would be in the mall during the middle of a work week. 
When thinking about how to sample, you have to ask yourself, 
“Who’s the target population for your study?” 
If you want to know the shopping habits of mall goers in general, you can’t just sample people who go to the mall during weekdays.

These people do not represent other people who work in the town. 
The key term here is represent.

So you have to think about who you are asking the question to, and who you can generalize those results to. 
It’s very important. 

There’re different types of biases when you run surveys. 
There’s selection bias. 
Let’s say you go to the city at 2am and you ask people to answer some survey questions. 
In this case, your sample is biased because you selected people who can be outside very late at night, 
and these people do not represent those who would be there during the day. 
But there’s more! 

In this example, there’s also nonresponse bias when people don't want to answer your question. 
Maybe many people would just ignore you and not answer your questions because it’s so late.

There's also response bias when you don’t word your question in a neutral way. 
If you ask people a question like, “What problems do you have with your colleague?”
People might feel pressure to come up with an answer, even though they might have no issues with their colleagues if you had asked “Do you have any problems with your colleague?” 

So saying something like “what problems do you have with your colleague” is a leading question that creates a response bias. 
Because the question is already assuming that the respondent has a problem with their colleague.
